                                               Abstract

         The rapid build-out of wind and solar power is essential to climate mitigation, yet vari-
      able output, congested networks, cyber risk, and unequal technical capacity make integra-
      tion difficult. At the same time, digital-sector loads (data centres, AI, and cryptocurrency)
      are becoming material and geographically concentrated. This report asks how a New Engi-
      neering approach can use AI without trading reliability, environmental integrity, security,
      or equity for speed. A structured desk review of international statistics, peer-reviewed
      research, Chinese policy, and two Chinese infrastructure cases was combined with a five-
      dimension coding framework. Four coupled gaps emerge: uncertainty is weakly connected
      to operational decisions; compute energy, water, and embodied carbon are under-counted;
      engineering, machine-learning, cyber, and public-interest capabilities remain separated;
      and data and standards are fragmented. China’s new-engineering policies, renewable scale,
      Zhangbei flexible direct-current demonstration, and East-to-West Computing project il-
      lustrate coordination across curriculum, infrastructure, and public standards. The report
      proposes a four-layer NE–AI Grid: physics-informed probabilistic intelligence with de-
      terministic fallbacks; practice-based interdisciplinary capability; carbon- and water-aware
      compute; and accountable, interoperable governance. A staged 60-month pilot adds in-
      dependent evaluation, cost disclosure, and equity gates. The framework gives utilities,
      universities, regulators, and communities shared acceptance tests and leaves institutional
      ownership local. The contribution is a China-informed but non-exclusive model that treats
      trustworthy AI as a socio-technical engineering capability rather than a software add-on.

1 Introduction
1.1 Background: an energy transition with a new load profile
The electricity system is being rebuilt around weather-dependent resources. Renewable capacity
reached about 3,870 GW at the end of 2023, with roughly 510 GW added that year (IRENA,
2024; IEA, 2024b). Variability raises the value of forecasting, flexibility, storage, transmission,
and capable institutions (IPCC, 2023).
     AI changes this problem in two ways. Models can learn links among weather, demand,
network state, and equipment condition, improving forecasts and maintenance. AI also belongs
to a growing digital-sector load: the IEA estimates that data centres, AI, and cryptocurrency
used roughly 460 TWh in 2022 (nearly 2% of global demand) and could exceed 1,000 TWh
by 2026 (IEA, 2024a). Without coordination, decarbonisation services can increase congestion,
water stress, and fossil back-up generation.
     The concrete global question is: how can countries integrate variable renewables and
AI-related loads while maintaining reliability, low lifecycle emissions, cybersecurity, and fair
access? It is also an education and policy question about who validates systems and bears risk.

1.2 Why a China-informed perspective matters
China is a consequential setting. It supplied over 50% of global renewable-capacity additions
in 2023, and the IEA expects it to account for almost 60% of new renewable capacity becoming
operational globally by 2028 (IEA, 2024b); renewable power capacity exceeds half of China’s
total installed power-generation capacity (National Energy Administration, 2024). This scale
tests coordination among power electronics, dispatch, digital infrastructure, and workforce.
     China’s policy response links education and infrastructure. The Ministry of Education’s
2017 response endorsed the university-led “Fudan Consensus” and “Tianda Action” and sup-
ported New Engineering projects built on interdisciplinary, industry-linked, outcome-based
learning (Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China, 2017; Ministry of Education,
2018b). National AI plans connect capability to “AI+X” education, while East-to-West Com-
puting links digital infrastructure to regional resources (State Council of the People’s Republic
of China, 2017; Ministry of Education, 2018a; National Development and Reform Commission,
2022). These mechanisms are not templates: transfer depends on local governance, markets,
data rights, and skills.
    Within China’s “Four New” agenda, the report focuses on New Engineering because
renewable-grid AI risks must be engineered, taught, and governed together.

1.3 Problem statement, questions, and objectives
Smart-grid debates often isolate forecast error from the conditions that make deployment safe
and durable; education debates celebrate interdisciplinarity without an operational test. We ask:
Engineering Trustworthy AI for Renewable-Powered Grids                                        2


1. RQ1: What coupled technical, environmental, workforce, and governance bottlenecks limit
   trustworthy AI-enabled integration of variable renewable electricity?

2. RQ2: What lessons can be learned from China’s new-engineering policy and renewable-
   grid/computing practices, and which lessons are transferable rather than context-bound?

3. RQ3: What implementable China-informed framework, pilot sequence, and evaluation met-
   rics can align reliability, decarbonisation, security, and equity?

     The objectives are to diagnose the coupled problem, connect Chinese practice to global
standards, and specify a falsifiable solution with accountable gates. The contribution is a tri-
angulated socio-technical analysis and pilot-ready curriculum–infrastructure–governance pack-
age.

2.3 Coding, comparison, and credibility
Each item was coded on five dimensions: forecast/dispatch; reliability/curtailment; compute
footprint; workforce/curriculum; and governance, cybersecurity, and equity. Metrics stayed
in original units because horizons, sites, baselines, and curtailment definitions differ. Statis-
tics required a denominator and year; project claims were labelled “project-reported” unless
independently evaluated; algorithmic gains were conditional when site or data dependence was
reported. A second pass rechecked all included records and numerical extractions; with one
reviewer, this is a consistency check rather than inter-rater reliability. No personal data were
collected.

2.4 Case selection
Two purposively selected Chinese cases expose different coordination problems: Zhangbei
links wind, solar, storage, and long-distance DC delivery, while East-to-West Computing links
data-centre hubs to regional power and land conditions. We compared them with NIST, IEC
62443, ISO/IEC 42001, and UNESCO/OECD principles (NIST, 2023; IEC, 2018; ISO, 2023;
UNESCO, 2021; OECD, 2019). They are illustrative, non-causal cases; comparison asks what
mechanism is observable and what adaptation a lower-capacity setting requires.

3.2 What the technical literature supports
The forecasting literature supports a direction, not a universal magnitude: deep-learning and
ensemble methods often reduce short-term error against persistence or single-model baselines
(Wang et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2020), but gains vary with horizon, weather, data, and market
rules. Distribution shift and sensor faults remain failure modes. Physics-informed learning can
impose conservation, power-flow, and equipment constraints (Karniadakis et al., 2021). In our
screened set, reliability, lifecycle carbon, water, cyber incidents, and distributional effects were
seldom reported together, encouraging narrow optimisation.

3.5 Robustness checks on the diagnosis
An exploratory qualitative check contrasted records with and without policy documents and
higher- and lower-income references. Higher-income records emphasised accuracy and cy-
ber controls; lower-income records emphasised connectivity, finance, and technician shortages.
Neither Chinese case proves that coordination lowers emissions or improves reliability, so the
pilot pre-registers baselines and reports null or adverse outcomes.


4 A China-Informed Solution: the NE–AI Grid Framework
4.1 Design principles
The framework turns the diagnosis into four principles:
Engineering Trustworthy AI for Renewable-Powered Grids                                      7


1. Safe uncertainty: enforce physical constraints, expose calibrated intervals, and retain a
   deterministic, human-authorised fallback.

2. Accounted capability: appraise compute energy, water, hardware, and carbon, and fund
   curricula, apprenticeships, and maintenance.

3. Open accountability: require auditability, red-teaming, portable interfaces, and incident
   disclosure.

4. Local adaptation: share Chinese mechanisms and benchmarks; local actors choose institu-
   tions and benefits.

4.2 Four layers and their interfaces
Figure 2 presents four mutually dependent layers with acceptance conditions: a model needs a
data and safety specification, a curriculum is assessed on a live system, and compute procure-
ment reports environmental and social indicators.

   1. Technical intelligence
   Physics-informed probabilistic forecasts; digital twins; constrained optimisa-
   tion or reinforcement learning; edge/cloud redundancy; deterministic fallback
   and operator authority.

   2. New-engineering capability

   3. Green compute

4.2.1 Layer 1: trustworthy technical intelligence
Layer 1 combines a physical baseline with probabilistic learning. A day-ahead model outputs
𝑝(𝑦 𝑡+ℎ | 𝑥𝑡 ) and calibrated intervals; a digital twin checks power-flow, ramp, voltage, and
Engineering Trustworthy AI for Renewable-Powered Grids                                           8


protection limits. Constrained dispatch treats reliability as a hard constraint and cost/emissions
as objectives. Operators retain authority; low confidence or lost connectivity triggers a tested
deterministic policy. Required artefacts are a sensor sheet, model card, seasonal hold-outs,
threat model, incident playbook, and versioned API. Federated learning does not replace access
control or validation.

4.2.2 Layer 2: new-engineering capability
Layer 2 organises a postgraduate track around a live grid problem: power markets and dynamics,
statistical learning, digital twins, cyber safety, lifecycle assessment, ethics, and communication.
Utility–university studios use anonymised data and hardware-in-the-loop tests; assessment re-
wards a safe operating envelope and reproducibility. Technicians, operators, procurement of-
ficers, and regulators receive stackable credentials. Chinese partners share benchmarks and
faculty exchange while host institutions retain control.

4.2.3 Layer 3: green compute
Layer 3 reports energy per training run and defined inference, WUE, PUE, hardware replace-
ment, and marginal grid carbon. A carbon-aware scheduler moves deferrable workloads subject
to latency, sovereignty, and reliability limits. Clean-power co-location requires published water
and community safeguards.

4.2.4 Layer 4: trust, standards, and global cooperation
Layer 4 assigns stewardship, access tiers, retention limits, and benefit-sharing through a data
trust or cooperative. Redacted model cards and incident logs are public; high-impact changes
trigger independent review and red-teaming. NIST, IEC 62443, ISO/IEC 42001, and UN-
ESCO/OECD principles supply compatible vocabularies (NIST, 2023; IEC, 2018; ISO, 2023;
UNESCO, 2021; OECD, 2019). Lower-income regions need concessional finance, offline tools,
and train-the-trainer partnerships.

     The utility owns safety and baseline KPIs; the university owns curriculum and benchmark
artefacts; procurement reports lifecycle cost; and a utility–university–regulator–worker–

estimates, confidence intervals, prediction-interval coverage, subgroup effects, and incidents

barred when outage risk, water stress, lifecycle cost, or exclusion exceeds its margin. Paid
apprenticeship seats and offline access are equity outputs.

4.4 Expected outcomes and theory of change
Near-term, operators receive calibrated information before action; medium-term outcomes are
lower curtailment and compute footprint; long-term outcomes are portable competence and
adaptable governance. The causal chain is conditional on validation, flexibility, accounting,
and participation.


5 Discussion and Conclusion
5.1 Answering the research questions
For RQ1, the principal barrier is coupling: uncertainty, physical constraints, compute external-
ities, fragmented skills, and unequal access interact. Point accuracy alone cannot show whether
deployment improves reliability or shifts risk.
      For RQ2, China’s mechanism-level lesson is coordination: New Engineering policy incen-
tivises boundary crossing, Zhangbei demonstrates physical co-design, and East-to-West Com-
puting makes compute location a planning variable. Administrative scale, procurement, and
finance are context-bound; transfer claims remain pilot hypotheses.
      For RQ3, NE–AI Grid is feasible because it starts bounded, retains human authority, and
ties scale to evidence. Its novelty is one acceptance test for education, green-compute account-
ing, governance, forecast, and dispatch.

5.2 Implications for China and the wider world
For China, the framework adds public evaluation and lifecycle disclosure to coordinated infras-
tructure. Programmes could require interoperable APIs, model cards, and auditable environ-
mental metrics, while universities assess operational competence. International collaboration
can share benchmarks, trainer studios, and finance for local data stewardship. The approach is
designed to be consistent with NIST, IEC, ISO/IEC, UNESCO, and OECD principles and can
operate through regional utilities.
      Trade-offs are explicit: carbon-aware scheduling can conflict with latency or data
sovereignty; federated learning can complicate debugging; and automation can precede
retraining. Each receives an owner and review date. Communities receive plain-language risk,
water, and benefit summaries plus a contest channel, making equity auditable.

5.3 Limitations and next steps
The study relies on public secondary sources that favour visible successes; project data and AI
metrics are not always comparable. Screening counts are a process record, not a probability
sample; heterogeneous definitions prevent pooling; and two cases cannot establish global trans-
fer. KPI thresholds require pre-registration and independent evaluation. Future work should
build a cross-climate benchmark, test reliability longitudinally, quantify water and embodied
Engineering Trustworthy AI for Renewable-Powered Grids                                    11


carbon, and study worker and community outcomes.

5.4 Conclusion
Renewable build-out and rising AI loads make grid integration a shared engineering challenge.
China’s experience suggests that scale matters when education, infrastructure, and standards
are coordinated. NE–AI Grid makes that mechanism testable through physics-aware fallbacks,
project-based capability, accounted compute impacts, and visible governance, offering a route
to a reliable and equitable digital transition.
